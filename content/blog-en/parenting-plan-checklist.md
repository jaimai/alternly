A **parenting plan** is the document that spells out how separated or divorced parents will raise their children across two homes: where the kids live and when, who makes which decisions, and how parents will handle everything from holidays to disagreements. Depending on where you live, it may be called a parenting plan, custody agreement, parenting agreement, or be part of a custody order.

Requirements differ from state to state. Some states have their own forms or required sections, and courts review plans based on the **best interests of the child**. But the questions a good plan answers are remarkably similar everywhere. Use this checklist to make sure you've thought through the essentials.

## 1. Legal custody and decision-making

Legal custody is about who makes major decisions for the children. Your plan should cover:

- Whether decision-making is **joint** or held by one parent (terminology varies by state)
- Which decisions are "major": typically **education, non-emergency health care and religious upbringing**, and sometimes extracurricular activities
- How joint decisions are made, and what happens if you can't agree
- Who can make **emergency medical decisions**, and how quickly the other parent is notified
- Access to school, medical and activity records for both parents

## 2. The regular parenting time schedule

This is the heart of most plans. Be specific:

- The **schedule type**, for example [week-on/week-off](/en/blog/week-on-week-off-custody-schedule), [2-2-3](/en/blog/2-2-3-custody-schedule), [2-2-5-5](/en/blog/2-2-5-5-custody-schedule), [every other weekend](/en/blog/every-other-weekend-custody-schedule), or a custom arrangement
- The **start date** and which parent starts the rotation
- **Exact handoff days and times** ("Friday after school" or "Sunday at 6 p.m.")
- Any **midweek visits** or phone/video call times
- Whether the schedule changes as children get older

## 3. Holidays and special days

Holiday time usually overrides the regular schedule. Include:

- A list of the holidays that matter to your family: Thanksgiving, winter break, Christmas Eve and Day, New Year's, spring break, Easter or other religious holidays, Fourth of July, Halloween, three-day weekends
- **How each is split**: alternated by even and odd years, divided within the day or break, or fixed to one parent
- **Start and end times** for each holiday period
- Mother's Day, Father's Day, the children's birthdays and parents' birthdays
- How winter break is labeled when it crosses into a new year

Our [holiday custody schedule guide](/en/blog/holiday-custody-schedule) covers common ways to split each one.

## 4. School breaks and summer

- How **summer** is divided (see our [summer custody schedule ideas](/en/blog/summer-custody-schedule))
- **Vacation weeks** for each parent, how many, and the deadline to choose them
- Who gets first pick, and whether that alternates by year
- How school break dates are determined each year (usually from the school district calendar)

## 5. Exchanges and transportation

- **Where exchanges happen**: school, one parent's home, a neutral public location
- **Who drives**, or how driving is split
- What happens if a parent is running late, and how long the other parent should wait
- Any conditions for exchanges (for example, exchanges at school only)

## 6. Travel and relocation

- **Notice required** for out-of-town trips during your own parenting time
- What information is shared: itinerary, where the children are staying, a contact number
- Rules for **out-of-state and international travel**, including passports and consent
- How a potential **move** by either parent will be handled. Many states have specific relocation rules, so this section especially benefits from legal advice.

## 7. Communication

- **How parents communicate**: email, text, a co-parenting app, or a combination
- Expected **response times** for non-urgent messages
- How and when children can contact the other parent (calls, video chats)
- A commitment to keep communication focused on the children. If communication is difficult, see our guide to [co-parenting with a difficult ex](/en/blog/co-parenting-with-a-difficult-ex).

## 8. Schedule changes and swaps

Life rarely follows a plan perfectly. Decide in advance:

- How to **request a swap** or schedule change, and how much notice is needed
- How swaps are **confirmed** (ideally in writing)
- Whether missed time is made up
- **Right of first refusal**: if a parent can't care for the kids during their time for a certain number of hours, is the other parent offered the time first?

## 9. Expenses

Child support is usually set separately, often following state guidelines. Your plan may still address:

- How **extra expenses** are shared: medical and dental costs not covered by insurance, school fees, childcare, extracurricular activities, camps
- The percentage split (50/50 or another share)
- How and when reimbursement requests are made, and what documentation is needed
- Who carries the children's **health insurance**

## 10. Parenting guidelines

Some families include shared agreements about:

- Keeping routines reasonably consistent across both homes (bedtime, screen time, homework)
- Not speaking negatively about the other parent in front of the children
- Introducing new partners
- Discipline approaches

Keep this section realistic. Rules you can't follow or enforce can create more conflict than they prevent.

## 11. Resolving disagreements and reviewing the plan

- A process for disputes before going to court, such as **mediation** or a parenting coordinator
- When the plan will be reviewed (for example, when a child starts middle school)
- How changes are made official. In many cases, informal agreements between parents don't change a court order; a family law attorney can explain what's needed in your state.

## Final tips

- **Be specific.** Vague language ("reasonable time," "the holidays") is where disputes start.
- **Think years ahead.** Kids grow, schools change and parents move. Build in flexibility and a review process.
- **Put it on a calendar.** A plan is only as good as both parents' ability to follow it day to day.

*This article is for informational purposes and isn't legal advice. Custody rules vary by state—talk to a family law attorney or mediator.*
