The **2-2-5-5 custody schedule** is a favorite among co-parents who want equal time *and* a predictable week. Instead of a rotation where every weekday shifts from one week to the next, each parent keeps the same two weekdays, and the weekends alternate. The result is a 50/50 schedule that's easier to plan around than a 2-2-3, with shorter separations than week-on/week-off.

Here's how it works, what a typical two weeks looks like, and how to tell whether it's right for your family.

## How the 2-2-5-5 schedule works

The name describes the blocks of time over a two-week cycle:

- **2 days** with Parent A (for example, Monday and Tuesday)
- **2 days** with Parent B (Wednesday and Thursday)
- **5 days** with Parent A (Friday through the following Tuesday)
- **5 days** with Parent B (Wednesday through Sunday)

Then the cycle starts over. The important detail: Parent A *always* has Mondays and Tuesdays, Parent B *always* has Wednesdays and Thursdays, and the Friday-to-Sunday weekend alternates between them.

## A two-week 2-2-5-5 example

| Day | Week 1 | Week 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Parent A | Parent A |
| Tuesday | Parent A | Parent A |
| Wednesday | Parent B | Parent B |
| Thursday | Parent B | Parent B |
| Friday | Parent A | Parent B |
| Saturday | Parent A | Parent B |
| Sunday | Parent A | Parent B |

Read it as a continuous timeline and you'll see the 2-2-5-5 shape: Parent A has Monday–Tuesday (2), Parent B has Wednesday–Thursday (2), Parent A has Friday of Week 1 through Tuesday of Week 2 (5), and Parent B has Wednesday through Sunday of Week 2 (5). Each parent ends up with **7 overnights out of 14**.

## Why parents like the 2-2-5-5 schedule

### Fixed weekdays make life easier to plan

If Parent A always has Mondays and Tuesdays, it's simple to schedule piano lessons, a recurring work shift, or a standing dinner with grandparents. Kids also tend to find it easier to remember: "Tuesday is Mom's day, Thursday is Dad's day."

### Weekends are shared fairly

Each parent gets every other weekend, and those weekends connect to the parent's fixed weekdays, creating one longer stretch of five days. That gives room for weekend trips without a mid-weekend handoff.

### Separations stay reasonably short

The longest time away from either parent is five days. For many school-age kids, that's a comfortable middle ground between the frequent moves of 2-2-3 and the full week of alternating weeks.

## The downsides to consider

### Still a fair number of exchanges

You'll typically have two to three handoffs a week. That's fewer than some rotations, but more than week-on/week-off.

### Five days can feel long for very young children

Babies and toddlers may do better with shorter stretches. Many families start with a [2-2-3 schedule](/en/blog/2-2-3-custody-schedule) and move to 2-2-5-5 as the kids grow.

### Proximity matters

Like any equal-time schedule with midweek exchanges, 2-2-5-5 works best when both homes are within a reasonable distance of school.

### Fixed weekdays can feel unequal

Sometimes one parent's fixed weekdays are the busier ones (the late practice night, the long workday). It's worth talking through which days each parent keeps before anything is written down.

## 2-2-5-5 vs. 2-2-3: what's the real difference?

Both are 50/50 schedules with alternating weekends. The difference is in the weekdays:

- In **2-2-3**, the weekday blocks swap every week, so Monday isn't always with the same parent.
- In **2-2-5-5**, the weekday blocks stay fixed; only the weekends alternate.

If predictability matters most, 2-2-5-5 usually wins. If shorter separations matter most, 2-2-3 usually wins.

## 2-2-5-5 vs. week-on/week-off

[Alternating weeks](/en/blog/week-on-week-off-custody-schedule) has the fewest transitions but means a full week apart from each parent. 2-2-5-5 is a compromise: more contact, a bit more coordination. Some families use 2-2-5-5 during the school year and switch to longer blocks over the summer (see our [summer custody schedule ideas](/en/blog/summer-custody-schedule)).

## Who is 2-2-5-5 a good fit for?

It often suits families where:

- Children are school-age and comfortable with a few days away from each parent
- Both parents have predictable weekly commitments
- Homes are close to school so midweek exchanges are practical
- Parents want equal time without a full week apart

Of course, courts decide custody based on the **best interests of the child**, and what that looks like can differ by family and by state. A schedule that works on paper still has to work for your kids.

## Practical tips for a smooth 2-2-5-5 rotation

1. **Use school as the exchange point when you can.** Drop-off by one parent, pick-up by the other keeps handoffs low-key.
2. **Name the handoff times precisely.** "After school Wednesday" or "6 p.m. Friday" prevents a lot of back-and-forth.
3. **Decide how holidays override the rotation.** Thanksgiving, winter break and spring break usually follow a separate [holiday custody schedule](/en/blog/holiday-custody-schedule).
4. **Agree on a swap process.** If one parent needs to trade a Tuesday for a Thursday, how is it asked, and how is it confirmed?
5. **Keep one source of truth.** A shared calendar avoids the "I thought it was my weekend" conversation.

## What to put in your parenting plan

For a 2-2-5-5 rotation, spell out which parent has which fixed weekdays, which parent has the first weekend and the exact start date, handoff times and places, and how holidays and school breaks take priority.
