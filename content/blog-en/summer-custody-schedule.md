Summer break changes everything about a custody schedule. School drop-offs disappear, camps and trips appear, and the rhythm that worked from September to June may not make sense for ten weeks of long days. That's why many parenting plans include a separate **summer custody schedule**.

Below are the most common summer arrangements, how to handle vacations and camps, and the details to agree on well before the last day of school.

## Why summer often needs its own schedule

During the school year, schedules are built around school: exchanges at drop-off, homework nights, bedtimes. In summer:

- Kids have more free time and often more activities (camps, sports, visits with relatives).
- Parents may want longer uninterrupted stretches for vacations or family trips.
- A parent with less time during the school year may want more time in the summer to balance things out.

A summer plan gives both parents something predictable to plan around.

## Summer schedule ideas

### 1. Keep the regular schedule, plus vacation weeks

The simplest option: your usual rotation continues all summer, and each parent gets one or two **vacation weeks** where the regular schedule pauses. This works well for younger children who do best with consistency.

### 2. Alternating two-week blocks

If you use [week-on/week-off](/en/blog/week-on-week-off-custody-schedule) during the school year, switching to two-week blocks in summer is a popular upgrade. Fewer exchanges and more room for trips. Some families add a midweek dinner so the kids don't go two full weeks without seeing the other parent.

### 3. Split summer in half

One parent has the first half of summer, the other has the second half, often alternating which half by year. This can suit families who live far apart, but it's a long stretch away from a parent, especially for younger children. Many families add visits or weekends for the "off" parent.

### 4. Extended summer for the weekend parent

When one parent has an [every other weekend schedule](/en/blog/every-other-weekend-custody-schedule) during the school year, summer is a chance to rebalance. For example, that parent might have several weeks (in one block or several) while the other parent gets alternating weekends.

### 5. A summer-only 2-2-5-5 or 2-2-3

Some parents with a less-than-equal school-year schedule move to an equal-time rotation like [2-2-5-5](/en/blog/2-2-5-5-custody-schedule) for the summer only. It gives both parents everyday time without long separations.

## Vacation weeks: rules worth writing down

Vacation time is where most summer conflicts start. A few clarifications can prevent them:

- **How many weeks each parent gets**, and whether they must be consecutive.
- **A deadline for choosing weeks**, for example, each parent submits their picks by a set date in the spring.
- **Who picks first**, often alternating by even and odd years, so each parent gets first choice every other year.
- **Notice for travel**: how far in advance an itinerary, destination and contact information are shared.
- **Out-of-state or international travel**: any consent or passport arrangements. Requirements can vary, so check what applies to you.

## Camps, activities and childcare

Summer camps and activities can cross over into both parents' time. It helps to agree on:

- **Who chooses and enrolls** in camps or programs, and whether both parents must agree.
- **How costs are shared.** Camp, childcare and activity fees add up quickly. Many parents split them 50/50 or in proportion to income; your parenting plan or child support order may already say how.
- **Transportation**: who drives to and from camp on each parent's days.
- **Sleepaway camps**: how they count against each parent's time.

## Holidays inside summer

The Fourth of July usually falls right in the middle of summer vacation plans. Decide whether it follows the summer schedule or your [holiday custody schedule](/en/blog/holiday-custody-schedule). Same goes for Father's Day (in June), birthdays, and family reunions.

## When does "summer" start and end?

It sounds obvious, but it's one of the most common points of confusion. Summer could mean:

- The day after the last day of school until the day before the first day of school, or
- A fixed date range, such as a set week in June to a set week in August.

School calendars change every year, so if you tie summer to the school calendar, check the new dates each year and add them to your shared calendar as soon as your district publishes them.

## Tips for a calmer summer

1. **Start planning in the spring.** Camps fill up and vacation plans need lead time.
2. **Put everything on one shared calendar**: vacation weeks, camps, travel days, and exchanges.
3. **Keep kids connected** to the other parent during long stretches with regular calls or video chats.
4. **Be flexible, but confirm in writing.** If you trade a week for a family reunion, make sure the swap is clearly agreed.
5. **Think about the kids' needs.** Courts decide based on the **best interests of the child**, and a summer schedule should work for your children's age, activities and friendships, not just for the adults.
