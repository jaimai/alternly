If you and your co-parent want to share time close to 50/50 but a full week apart feels too long for your kids, the **2-2-3 custody schedule** is probably on your shortlist. It's one of the most common equal-time rotations in the US, especially for younger children, and it's also one of the most confusing to picture the first time you see it written down.

This guide walks through exactly how a 2-2-3 rotation works, shows a two-week example, and lays out the honest pros and cons so you can decide whether it fits your family.

## What is a 2-2-3 custody schedule?

A 2-2-3 schedule splits each week into three blocks:

- **2 days** with Parent A
- **2 days** with Parent B
- **3 days** (usually the weekend) with Parent A

The following week, the pattern flips: Parent B starts with two days, Parent A has the next two, and Parent B gets the three-day weekend. Over any two-week period, each parent has the children for **7 overnights**, which makes it a true equal-time arrangement.

The key idea is that no child goes more than three days without seeing each parent. That's why many families with babies, toddlers and early elementary kids start here.

## A two-week 2-2-3 example

Here's the most common version, with handoffs on Monday, Wednesday and Friday:

| Day | Week 1 | Week 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Parent A | Parent B |
| Tuesday | Parent A | Parent B |
| Wednesday | Parent B | Parent A |
| Thursday | Parent B | Parent A |
| Friday | Parent A | Parent B |
| Saturday | Parent A | Parent B |
| Sunday | Parent A | Parent B |

Notice that in Week 1, Parent A has Monday–Tuesday *and* Friday–Sunday. Then in Week 2, it's Parent B's turn for both blocks. That means **each parent gets every other weekend**, and the weekday blocks rotate too.

Some families adjust the handoff days (for example, switching on Sunday evening instead of Monday morning) so exchanges happen at school or daycare. The structure stays the same: 2, 2, 3, then flip.

## Pros of the 2-2-3 schedule

### Frequent contact with both parents

This is the big one. Young children often do better when they see both parents regularly. With 2-2-3, the longest stretch away from either parent is three days.

### Equal time, including weekends

Both parents get weekday routines (homework, bedtime, school mornings) *and* weekend fun. Neither parent becomes "the weekend parent" or "the school-night parent."

### Handoffs can happen at school

If you schedule exchanges at drop-off and pick-up, the kids go to school from one home and come back to the other. That can take a lot of tension out of transitions, especially if face-to-face exchanges are hard.

## Cons of the 2-2-3 schedule

### Lots of transitions

There are roughly three handoffs a week. For some kids that's reassuring; for others, especially older kids with busy schedules, it can feel like living out of a backpack.

### The weekday blocks move every week

Because the pattern flips, "Mondays" aren't always with the same parent. That can make it harder to plan recurring activities, work shifts or standing commitments. A shared calendar helps a lot here.

### It works best when parents live close

With frequent exchanges, a long commute between homes (or between one home and school) adds up quickly. Families who live far apart often find a longer rotation more realistic.

### It requires steady communication

More handoffs mean more small coordination moments: the forgotten soccer cleats, the library book, the permission slip. It works well for parents who can keep communication brief and practical.

## Who is the 2-2-3 schedule best for?

There's no one-size-fits-all answer, and courts in every state look at what's in the **best interests of the child** rather than a single "right" schedule. That said, 2-2-3 is often a good fit when:

- Your children are young (infants through early elementary) and do better with short separations
- Both homes are close to each other and to school or daycare
- Both parents want equal parenting time, including weekends
- You can coordinate calmly on small day-to-day details

As kids get older, many families "graduate" from 2-2-3 to a [2-2-5-5 schedule](/en/blog/2-2-5-5-custody-schedule) (same parent on the same weekdays every week) or to [alternating weeks](/en/blog/week-on-week-off-custody-schedule) (fewer transitions).

## 2-2-3 vs. 2-2-5-5 vs. week-on/week-off

- **2-2-3**: most transitions, shortest time apart, weekdays rotate every week.
- **2-2-5-5**: each parent keeps the same two weekdays every week, weekends alternate. Fewer surprises for activities.
- **Week-on/week-off**: fewest transitions, but seven days away from each parent.

All three give each parent 50% of overnights. The difference is really about rhythm: how often the kids move, and how predictable the week feels.

## Tips to make a 2-2-3 rotation work

1. **Pick handoff times that match your kids' day.** School pick-up is often smoother than a doorstep exchange at 7 p.m.
2. **Keep duplicate basics in both homes.** Toothbrushes, pajamas, chargers and a spare set of school supplies cut down on forgotten-item texts.
3. **Write down how holidays override the rotation.** Your regular schedule and your [holiday custody schedule](/en/blog/holiday-custody-schedule) should say clearly which one wins.
4. **Decide how day swaps work.** Life happens. Agree on how far ahead a swap should be requested and how it's confirmed, so nobody is left wondering what was agreed.
5. **Use one shared calendar.** With weekday blocks that flip every week, a calendar both parents can check beats trying to remember whose Wednesday it is.

## Putting it in your parenting plan

If you're drafting or updating a parenting plan, be specific: the start date, which parent begins the rotation, handoff days and times, the exchange location, and how the regular schedule interacts with holidays and school breaks.
